🎵 Tone Changes — written vs. spoken

Pinyin tone marks show the dictionary tone — but in fast, natural speech a few common patterns change how a syllable actually comes out. These are called tone sandhi.

1. Third tone + third tone → second + third
When two 3rd-tone syllables sit next to each other, the first one is spoken as a 2nd tone.
Written: 你好 nǐ hǎo  →  Spoken: ní hǎo

2. The "half" third tone
Before a 1st, 2nd, or 4th tone, a 3rd tone is often just a low dip with no rise back up — lighter than the full dip-and-rise you hear when a 3rd tone is said alone.

3. 一 (yī) changes
Alone, or at the end of a word, 一 stays 1st tone (yī).
Before a 4th tone, it becomes 2nd tone: 一个 yí gè.
Before a 1st, 2nd, or 3rd tone, it becomes 4th tone: 一天 yì tiān, 一年 yì nián, 一起 yì qǐ.

4. 不 (bù) changes
Stays 4th tone (bù) before a 1st, 2nd, or 3rd tone.
Becomes 2nd tone before another 4th tone: 不是 bú shì, 不对 bú duì.

5. Neutral tone (轻声)
Some syllables lose their tone entirely and go light/toneless — grammar particles like 的, 了, 吗, 呢, and the second syllable of many everyday words such as 谢谢 xièxie or 妈妈 māma.

This app's pinyin and audio always reflect the written/dictionary tone — worth knowing these patterns so spoken Mandarin doesn't feel "off" from what you've memorized.

部 Radicals — memorizing characters faster

A radical (部首) is a recurring building-block inside a character. Most characters are a semantic-phonetic compound: one part hints at meaning (the radical), the other hints at sound. Learning to spot radicals turns an intimidating character into a small, memorable combination instead of a random shape.

Radical Meaning Examples

How to use this: when you meet a new character, ask "what's the left/top part, and what's the right/bottom part?" Often one piece is a radical you already know (a meaning clue), and the other piece sounds similar to another character you know (a sound clue). Spotting the pattern sticks far better than memorizing the character as an unrelated picture.

Tip: while practicing on the Flashcards tab, glance at repeated components across different words — the same radicals keep showing up.
